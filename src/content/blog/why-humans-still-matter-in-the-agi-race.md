Every quarter for the last two years, someone has declared that "this is the model that changes everything." Sometimes they were half right. What actually changed, quietly and unevenly, wasn't raw capability — it was the range of tasks a model could be trusted to run without a human re-checking every step.

That shift matters more than the benchmark charts suggest, because it moves the bottleneck. When a model could only draft a paragraph, the human's job was authorship. When a model can draft, revise, and ship a first version of a report end to end, the human's job becomes something closer to editorial judgment: deciding what's worth building, catching the confidently wrong claim buried in paragraph four, and knowing when "good enough" isn't.

## The skill that doesn't automate away

Prompting is not the durable skill here. Prompting techniques have a shelf life measured in product releases. What doesn't decay is the ability to specify a problem clearly enough that a system — human or machine — can't quietly misunderstand it. That's a management skill wearing an engineering costume.

Teams that are actually getting leverage from AI right now share one habit: they write down what "done" looks like before they hand off the task, whether the one doing the task is a junior hire or a model. Everyone else is spending their time reviewing output against a standard that only exists in their head.

## What this means for the next year

- **Review capacity becomes the constraint**, not generation capacity. If your team can produce ten drafts an hour but can only responsibly review two, you've built a bottleneck, not a superpower.
- **Domain judgment appreciates.** The people who know *why* an answer is wrong, not just that it looks wrong, are the ones whose time gets more valuable as generation gets cheaper.
- **"AI-native" is a workflow redesign, not a tool subscription.** Bolting a chat window onto an unchanged process mostly just adds a step.

None of this is an argument against urgency — the tooling is moving fast enough that waiting a year to adopt it is a real cost. It's an argument for where to point the urgency: not at chasing the newest model, but at building the review habits and specification discipline that make any model useful.
